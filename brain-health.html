Brain health and hearing loss

Research continues to show a strong connection between untreated hearing loss, cognitive load, social isolation, and dementia risk. The good news: treating hearing loss is one of the most actionable ways to support long-term brain health.

Schedule a hearing consultation View risk factors

Why hearing can affect the brain

When hearing is unclear, your brain has to work harder to make sense of speech. Over time, that extra effort can leave you feeling mentally drained. And when conversations become exhausting, many people naturally start to avoid social situations — which reduces the brain stimulation that helps keep your mind sharp.

If you’ve noticed you’re straining to keep up, asking people to repeat themselves, or avoiding noisy environments, it may be time to get your hearing checked.

Common dementia risk factors that hearing care can support

Cognitive load (your brain works harder)

With hearing loss, your brain spends more energy “filling in the blanks.” Many people describe feeling tired after conversations or avoiding restaurants because it’s exhausting.

Auditory deprivation

If the brain doesn’t receive clear sound for a long time, it can adapt to “less input.” Treating hearing earlier can make adaptation easier.

Social withdrawal and isolation

When hearing becomes work, people often pull back from group events, family dinners, and social activities. Supporting communication helps keep you connected.

Balance and fall risk

Hearing loss is associated with a higher risk of falls. Better hearing can improve environmental awareness, and we can also screen balance when needed.
