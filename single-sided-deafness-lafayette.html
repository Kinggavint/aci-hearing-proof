What single-sided deafness is

Single-sided deafness (SSD) means severe or profound hearing loss in one ear with normal or near-normal hearing in the other. Some people wake up one morning and cannot hear from one side (that is called sudden sensorineural hearing loss and it is a medical emergency). Others lose hearing gradually over months or years. And some people are born with SSD.

The technical name is asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, or unilateral hearing loss. Patients often describe it as feeling like their world has gone flat: they cannot tell where sounds are coming from, they cannot follow conversations in a busy restaurant, and they turn their whole head just to hear the person on their bad side.

SSD is different from bilateral hearing loss. Two hearing aids will not fix it because the deaf ear cannot process sound anymore. SSD needs a different toolkit.

Common causes of single-sided deafness

  • Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL). Hearing loss that develops over 72 hours or less. This is a medical emergency. If it happens to you, get to an ENT or emergency department within 48 hours. Steroid treatment in the first 2 weeks can sometimes restore hearing.
  • Acoustic neuroma. A benign tumor on the hearing and balance nerve. It grows slowly and often first shows up as one-sided hearing loss or one-sided tinnitus. An MRI is usually the definitive test.
  • Viral infections. Mumps, measles, viral labyrinthitis, and even severe cases of COVID have been linked to sudden one-sided hearing loss.
  • Ototoxic medication exposure. Some chemotherapy drugs (cisplatin), certain IV antibiotics (aminoglycosides), and long-term high-dose aspirin can damage the cochlea. Damage is sometimes worse on one side.
  • Head trauma. Skull fractures involving the temporal bone can sever the auditory nerve or damage the cochlea on one side.
  • Meniere disease. Episodic vertigo, tinnitus, and fluctuating hearing loss that usually starts unilateral before progressing.
  • Congenital. Some children are born with normal hearing in one ear and no functional hearing in the other, often due to cochlear malformations or nerve anomalies.

How single-sided deafness affects daily life

SSD is not just a hearing problem. It is a spatial problem, a fatigue problem, and a safety problem.

  • Sound localization is gone. Your brain uses tiny time and volume differences between your two ears to know where sound is coming from. With SSD, that stereo comparison is broken. Ambulance sirens, honking cars, someone calling your name from across a room. You cannot tell which direction any of it is coming from.
  • Speech in noise gets very hard. The good ear on its own does not have enough information to separate speech from background noise. Restaurants, family gatherings, and church services become exhausting.
  • The head-shadow effect. Sounds coming from your deaf side lose about 15 decibels of loudness by the time they reach your good ear (your head physically blocks them). You miss half of the conversation.
  • Listening fatigue. Your brain works overtime trying to fill in the missing information. By evening you are wiped out.
  • Safety concerns. Not hearing traffic from one side, missing alarms, missing the beeping of a smoke detector across the house.

Four treatment paths for single-sided deafness

Which one fits you depends on the cause of your SSD, whether your deaf ear can still process sound with the right stimulation, and how much of a change you are willing to make. ACI walks you through all four during your evaluation.

1. CROS hearing aid (Contralateral Routing of Signal)

A wireless system with a small transmitter worn on your deaf ear and a hearing aid worn on your good ear. The transmitter picks up sound and streams it to the hearing aid, so the good ear hears sound coming from both sides. No surgery. Same-day fitting. Rechargeable. Works for most SSD patients whose good ear has normal or near-normal hearing.

Best fit: profound loss on one side, normal or near-normal hearing on the other side, and a patient who does not want any surgery.

2. BiCROS hearing aid

Same wireless setup as CROS, but the aid on the good ear also amplifies sound because the good ear itself has some hearing loss. A CROS transmitter alone will not help someone whose better ear also needs amplification. BiCROS solves both problems at once.

Best fit: profound loss on one side, mild-to-moderate hearing loss on the other side.

3. Bone-anchored hearing aid (BAHA)

A device that sends sound from your deaf side through the bone of your skull to the working inner ear on your good side. Requires a small titanium implant behind the ear (outpatient procedure, 30 to 45 minutes). Some patients prefer the sound quality of a BAHA to a CROS system because it uses the natural bone-conduction pathway rather than an over-the-air wireless link. A non-surgical softband version is available for a trial before you commit to the surgical implant, and is the standard option for children under 5.

Best fit: patients who want a longer-term device, do not mind a small surgery, and either dislike the sound of CROS during their trial or need a solution that does not require pairing two devices every day.

4. Cochlear implant on the deaf ear

The only treatment that restores hearing to the deaf ear itself. A cochlear implant bypasses the damaged parts of the ear and stimulates the hearing nerve directly. Since 2019 the FDA has approved cochlear implants specifically for single-sided deafness in adults, and Cochlear Corporation followed with FDA clearance in 2022. Not every SSD patient is a candidate. Best candidates: those who lost hearing after developing language (post-lingual), those with recent hearing loss (typically under 10 years), and those with a healthy auditory nerve on the deaf side. ACI performs the full candidacy evaluation and refers to a regional surgical center for the implant procedure.

Best fit: patients who want true bilateral hearing back, are within the FDA candidacy criteria, and are willing to commit to a longer rehabilitation and mapping process after surgery.

How ACI evaluates single-sided deafness

An SSD evaluation at ACI takes about 90 minutes and covers:

  1. Case history. When did the hearing loss start, was it sudden or gradual, any triggering event, any tinnitus or vertigo, any prior medical workup.
  2. Otoscopy and tympanometry. Look inside the ears and confirm the middle ear is working normally (SSD is a cochlear or nerve issue, not an eardrum issue).
  3. Pure-tone audiometry. Establish thresholds across all frequencies in both ears.
  4. Speech recognition testing. Word recognition scores in quiet and in noise. This is the number that predicts how much any treatment will help.
  5. Otoacoustic emissions. Test outer hair cell function in the cochlea. Helps localize where in the ear the damage is.
  6. Aided testing. The key step. You try a CROS demo, a BiCROS demo (if appropriate), and a BAHA softband demo in the same session. We measure speech-in-noise scores with each option so you and your audiologist can see which one measurably helps most.
  7. Referral pathway. If cochlear implant candidacy is a real consideration, we set up a follow-up appointment with a regional surgical center for imaging and surgical consultation.

You walk out of this appointment with three things: a diagnosis, a recommended treatment path with a measurable reason behind the recommendation, and a written cost estimate for whatever path you pick.

Regional referral pathway for BAHA and cochlear implants

ACI performs the diagnostic workup, aided testing, and candidacy determination in Lafayette. Surgical procedures for BAHA implantation and cochlear implantation happen at regional surgical centers in south Louisiana. After surgery you return to ACI for:

  • Device activation, typically 4 to 6 weeks after surgery
  • Programming and mapping sessions (multiple visits over the first 6 to 12 months)
  • Ongoing follow-up and adjustment for the life of the device

This split model means you get a single team managing your long-term audiology care right here in Lafayette while the specialty surgery happens at a center that performs it every week. Call us at 337-223-9448 to talk through the referral steps for your specific situation.

Cost of single-sided deafness treatment

  • CROS or BiCROS treatment plan: falls inside our standard treatment plan pricing of $4,500 to $7,500. Includes the transmitter, the hearing aid, real-ear measurement fitting, follow-up adjustments, cleanings, and our care commitment for the life of your devices.
  • BAHA: the surgical implant and the sound processor are usually billed separately. Sound processor pricing is similar to a mid-tier premium hearing aid. Many insurance plans cover the surgical procedure and the device when medically justified. ACI verifies coverage before you commit.
  • Cochlear implant: the device itself is almost always covered by Medicare, most commercial insurance, and Louisiana Medicaid because it is a medically necessary implanted device. Out-of-pocket cost depends on your deductible and copay structure. We verify benefits before your surgical consult.
